# Overview

This skill guides copy and prose toward natural, conversational language. Use it whenever writing or reviewing text that will be read by a person — product copy, landing pages, UI labels, onboarding text, emails, or any prose where sounding human matters. The core goal is to strip out patterns that mark text as AI-generated or over-produced and replace them with writing that sounds like a person actually said it.

## What this sounds like

- **Conversational, not punchy.** Use commas and natural sentence flow rather than a series of short declarative fragments. Short sentences have their place, but stringing too many together reads as either breathless or self-important. Let ideas connect.
- **Honest and low-key.** Don't oversell. The app does what it does, and the copy should describe it plainly. If something is genuinely useful, say so simply. Avoid words that strain too hard to sound exciting: "powerful," "seamless," "transformative," "game-changing."
- **Friendly, not casual.** The tone is warm and approachable, but not slangy. Write the way you'd explain something to a friend who asked how the app works.
- **No drama.** Avoid rhetorical questions used as setup ("Want to actually learn Japanese?"), stacked em-dashes for emphasis, or sentence fragments meant to sound bold. These patterns are recognizable as copywriting moves and erode trust.

## What to avoid

- **Em-dashes as drama.** An em-dash is fine occasionally to set apart a parenthetical thought, but using it to add punch ("The goal is learning, not failing") reads as AI-generated or overly produced. Use a comma, rephrase the sentence, or split it naturally instead.
- **"Actually."** It implies the reader had the wrong idea, which comes across as condescending. Cut it almost everywhere.
- **Fragmented sentences used for effect.** "The rhythm of real texting." "Fast. Focused. Effective." This style feels like it's trying too hard. Write complete thoughts.
- **Tech-bro framing.** Avoid anything that sounds like a pitch deck: "two modes, one powerful system," "recognition-first methodology," "optimized for retention." Plain English works better.
- **Hedging qualifiers.** "Quite," "very," "really," "truly." They weaken what they're meant to strengthen.

## Four small changes for UI copy

These come from Apple's UX writing team (WWDC25, "Make a big impact with small writing changes") and apply directly to app copy: notifications, error messages, instructional text, buttons.

- **Cut filler words.** Don't fill space just because it's there — your app doesn't have a minimum word count, and usually the opposite is true. Look hard at adverbs and adjectives ("simply," "quickly," "easily," "fast," "simple"). If the word doesn't add real information, cut it: "Simply enter your license plate number to quickly pay for parking" just becomes "Enter your license plate number to pay for parking" — no clarity lost. Keep a descriptive word only when it clarifies actual behavior (e.g., "automatically" in "Feed your pets automatically" tells someone the app runs on a schedule, not just on demand). Also cut interjections ("uh oh," "oops," "oh no") and pleasantries ("sorry," "thank you," "please") when they don't add meaning — in an error or delay message they read as insincere or make light of the problem. Watch for exclamation points doing the same job.
- **Avoid repetition.** Don't say the same thing twice in different words. A notification headline and body that both restate "you're late" and "10 minutes" can collapse into one line: "Delivery delayed 10 minutes." UX writing is an economy-of-language exercise — look for ideas that overlap and merge them.
- **Lead with the why.** State the benefit or reason before the instruction, not after: "To get reservation updates, enter your phone number" lands better than "Enter your phone number to get reservation updates." This matters most in error messages, push notifications, and instructional copy/tips — the shortcut is "to do or get one thing, first do another thing," with the payoff up front.
- **Keep a word list.** For each app, track which term you use for a given concept and which synonyms you're deliberately avoiding (e.g., "alias" — not "handle," "user name," or "title"), plus a short definition if it's ambiguous. This is what keeps button labels and terminology consistent across a whole app. Build it incrementally — one word at a time is fine — rather than trying to write it all at once. When unsure what term to use for a platform-specific concept, check the Apple Style Guide.

## A quick test

Read the copy out loud. If it sounds like something you'd say to a friend, it's probably right. If it sounds like a slide or a tweet thread, rewrite it. Reading aloud is also the fastest way to catch filler and repetition — you'll hear the extra words before you can point to them.
